# Case Study Voice — Ben Yamron

Concrete rules for writing case-study copy, distilled from Ben's own edits. This is the practical companion to the Voice section in `guidelines.md` — that's the philosophy; this is the checklist. It grows every time Ben reacts to a draft.

**Litmus test before any line ships:** read it aloud. If it sounds like a motivational poster, a press release, or something an AI wrote to sound impressive — rewrite it. It should sound like Ben explaining his work to a peer who already knows the field: personable, honest, direct, unhurried.

---

## What the copy exists to signal

Ben's words: keep it "concise without sacrificing meaning or signal on my design / critical thinking / systems thinking / builder skill." Every sentence should either prove one of those or set up the one that does. If it does neither, cut it. High signal, low effort — the reader should be able to scan it and learn something.

---

## Cut these (each is a real edit)

- **AI-speak / motivational-poster lines.** The tell: it's a tidy aphorism that could sit under any project.
  - ✗ "The one thing I won't trade for speed is quality." → ✓ "The challenge is holding quality steady as I hand off more of the work."
  - ✗ "The hard part wasn't building it — it was keeping it true." → ✓ "It was important to keep it up to date."
- **Clever-clever aphorisms.** ✗ "A dashboard that shows everything shows nothing." → ✓ "Most apps show you everything and leave you to find what matters."
- **Precious / poetic justifications.** ✗ "reads the room without saying a word," "which is exactly why it earns its place." Say the plain thing instead.
- **Performative over-explanation.** Don't walk through a spec to look rigorous. Ben on the heart-rate chart: "it feels performative to overexplain it" — state the principle, give one example, move on.
- **False aha moments.** Don't inflate MVP scope or routine craft into a revelation. Ben on the observing-voice example: "i don't think that's a huge aha moment like it's made out to be." Only spotlight decisions that genuinely reveal judgment.
- **Table-stakes bragging.** Built with AI, uses an LLM, prototyped in code — assume the reader does this too. "that's table stakes, not noteworthy anymore." Mention only if the *how* is the insight.
- **False differentiators.** Don't frame a scoping choice as a design principle. "Few metrics" was MVP scope, not the point — the differentiator was the UX, not the smaller feature set.
- **Repetition / beating a dead horse.** Once a limitation or context is set, move on. Ben: "it beats a dead horse on the fact that i'm not reading the code." One light touch, not five.
- **Overselling / spin.** Be honest about scale and state. Ben: "i don't want to oversell — this doesn't have a ton of users." Understating reads as more credible than inflating. Name the real constraint (in progress, small usage, MVP).

---

## Rules of thumb

- **Write for insiders.** The audience is senior and AI-native; don't explain what they already know. Ben: "write this as more for insiders."
- **Plain English over metaphor.** The more literal, the more trustworthy. Three words beat ten fancy ones.
- **One example per point, then stop.** Concrete beats comprehensive.
- **Lead with the decision or the insight, not the process.**
- **If it could appear on any designer's portfolio unchanged, cut or sharpen it.**
- **Shorter is usually right.** Nearly every round of Ben's feedback asks for tighter — start terse.
